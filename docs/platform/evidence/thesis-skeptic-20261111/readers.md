# Three readers

Each pass reads the live page of 2026-11-11 top to bottom as one kind of
reader, using only what that reader could open: the page, its links and
public data. Row numbers point into `claims.md`; source ids into
`sources.md`. These are rehearsals written by the builder, not interviews.
The six-person cold-reader test (ZOD-42) was not run.

## 1. The hostile journalist

Reads the link preview first, then the note, then looks for who is behind it
and what they stand to gain.

**Where they stop believing**

1. The third paragraph. "In July 2024 twelve coins appeared on Solana" and
   then "We run the registry that lists them." Coins do not appear. The page
   never says who made them. One click on any origin receipt shows a single
   wallet created all twelve in 44 minutes (S10). The appendix then speaks of
   "the operator's existing infrastructure at deployment time" (N5) while the
   disclosure page says the operator's statement "does not establish who
   controls token deployment" (S6). From here on they read every "we" and
   every "community" as evasion.
2. "The community that formed around these coins in 2024 ... built
   Zodiacs.org" (H3). The About page says a company formed on 11 August 2026
   owns and runs it, "maintained by automated systems, without a traditional
   human editorial staff" (S8).
3. The survival chart (E12). They know what "apples to oranges" looks like.

**What they would quote against us**

- "Most memecoins stop trading the day they are made. Twelve of them ... are
  in their third year." Set beside CoinGecko's own sentence that coins
  trading on Raydium "would be incorrectly classified" as dead, and the fact
  that these twelve trade only on Raydium and were never on Pump.fun.
- "We kept the critics too." Set beside the receipt, which is a defender
  saying "Bubblemaps is FUD btw" (G15, G18).
- "No adverse figure is omitted." Set beside the figures that are omitted:
  about $51,000 to $131,000 of market value per coin, about $0.73 million for
  all twelve, and 52 trades across all twelve in a day (S12, K15).
- The project account's reply to Portnoy on the day: "Website is going to
  make it seamless for people to invest in either $LIBRA, or any of the other
  11 zodiac signs" (S4), beside the page's "not advice" and "promises no
  yield, buyback, price, or roadmap".
- The Portnoy quotation cut before "It may rip" and "It's a collectible"
  (G12), and Portnoy's "I gotta be largest share holder!" an hour later.
- "We hold some of these coins", with no amount, beside "Project treasury:
  None" with a "Verify" link that verifies nothing (K12).
- "No one appointed these digital assets 'official'", beside the navigation's
  "Official Zodiac token collection" (I1).
- "We missed our own deadline" (J1). Honest, and quotable.

**What they would share**

- The risk paragraph: "A memecoin can go to zero, and these can too."
  "Less than the price of a lunch." "No outside app has adopted them yet."
- "We would rather you checked our claim than took it on trust", but only
  with a note on how the checking went.
- The YouGov pair, 90% and 27%. It is true and it is a good line.

## 2. The crypto skeptic

Pastes the Leo address into an explorer and an aggregator before reading
past the first chart.

**Where they stop believing**

1. The fourth bar of the survival chart (E12). "12 of 12 still trading on day
   858" beside Pump.fun's 5%. They check: the coins were minted directly, not
   on Pump.fun; "trading" is a handful of Jupiter-routed swaps a day, some of
   them one wallet passing through two zodiac pools in one transaction (S10);
   the pool shares were burned, so the pools cannot be pulled and will show
   some routed volume for as long as Solana runs. Survival of that kind says
   nothing about demand. The essay's "we think the reason is their subject"
   (F2) ignores this.
2. "The ten largest accounts hold between 23% and 31%" (H10). The site's own
   public file has newer and worse numbers, and has not been refreshed since
   24 August. A "weekly snapshot" that stopped eleven weeks ago reads as a
   monitor someone switched off.
3. "Project treasury: None" (K6, K12, K20). The operator holds coins, will
   not say how many, and the "Verify" link on the aggregate row opens the
   wallet that created all twelve.

**What they would quote against us**

- The numbers the page does not print: each coin is worth about $51,000 to
  $131,000 in total; pools hold about $11,000 to $19,000; a day's trading
  across all twelve is under $700 (S12). The 2024 posts the page cites as
  receipts put Cancer at $444,000 and Leo at $265,000 in the first week (S4),
  so the receipts themselves show the fall.
- "about 2,800 wallets hold Leo alone" (H2): token accounts, dust included,
  pool included.
- "any app can recognise the listed Leo without asking us ... a dating profile
  could show it" beside "No outside app has adopted them yet" and an SDK whose
  593 monthly downloads are, by the page's own evidence file, not evidence of
  a consumer.
- "first documented trading (unaffiliated ...)" (L9), where the receipt is a
  holder posting "Coins are up about 2x" and counting a thirteenth coin,
  "$ZODIAC", that the essay never mentions. Jupiter's index reports
  `devMints: 24` for each of the twelve (S12). They will ask what the other
  coins were.
- "Each instrument row links to an independently checkable receipt, or is
  labeled operator-attested or pending" (N10). No row is labelled.
- "LP >= 92% burned" as an aggregate when five of twelve rows give no
  percentage (K10).
- "Astrofolio.xyz redirects there." It redirects to the home page (L3). A
  small thing, checked in five seconds, that tells them nobody reran the
  checks.

**What they would share**

- Mint and freeze authority are null on all twelve, and the supply figures
  match the ledger to the last decimal (K7, K8). That part is clean.
- The Portnoy post. The address match is real and it is a good story.
- "A partial burn stays partial." "These rules do not protect the price."
- The missed-deadline notice, as an example of a project saying so.

## 3. The working astrologer

Reads for whether the writer knows the subject, and for whether the essay
treats astrology as a practice or as a market.

**Where they stop believing**

1. "this one has held that position for about 2,400 years" (C4), followed by
   "The transmission" (D8 to D12). The twelve equal signs are about 2,400
   years old, but "known by nearly everyone" is a twentieth-century fact, a
   product of the sun-sign column. The essay's own source says Naylor's 1930
   piece was a birth chart and that he reached "star signs" only "By 1937"
   (S5), and it quotes an astrologer calling the result "this vast
   over-simplification of a noble, ancient art". The page uses the 1930 date
   as if the twelve-sign column began then. The tablet date, "c. 450 BCE",
   is half a century early (S16). In the drawer, the Neo-Babylonian empire is
   shown as a "carrier" of the fixed twelve although it fell in 539 BCE,
   before the fixed twelve existed (M11).
2. The hover times on the attention chart (F8). Ingress times printed to the
   millisecond, and the Cancer ingress is about a minute off the published
   solstice (S15a). An astrologer works with these times. A site that sells
   itself on exact charts should not print three false decimal places, and
   the sign names are in lower case.
3. "A meme about Scorpio is about you." The whole essay equates "your sign"
   with the Sun sign. A practitioner's first lesson to a client is that the
   Sun sign is one placement among many. A Leo token for "a Leo" flattens
   the chart to the newspaper column the essay has just described.

**What they would quote against us**

- "Almost everyone knows their sign. Few say they believe." (C5). They would
  note that 27% is not "few", and that the essay is content to sell to the
  90% while keeping its distance from the 27% and from the people who
  practise.
- "People come for the astrology. The coins sit one labelled link away."
  (H6, H7), read as: the free readings are the top of a funnel.
- "Coins are its newest form" (D15), beside a history of tablets, manuscripts
  and almanacs, all of which carried knowledge. A coin carries a name.
- The site's About line that it is "maintained by automated systems, without
  a traditional human editorial staff" (S8), if they follow the link.

**What they would share**

- The Naylor and Princess Margaret story, with the Smithsonian link.
- The Unicode plate: the twelve glyphs, in Unicode since June 1993 (M8).
- The Wikipedia reads chart with ingresses and eclipses marked (F6, F7). The
  peak three days after the Aries ingress is a real and pleasing detail.
- The free tools themselves, more than the essay.

## Problems all three readers hit, ranked

Ranked by how early in the page the reader meets the problem and how much of
the argument rests on it. Aimed at the rewrite (ZOD-36 to ZOD-39) and the
live edition.

1. **The survival comparison (A1, A2, E5, E10, E12).** It is the link
   preview, a chart heading and the hinge of the argument. The journalist
   sees a rigged comparison, the crypto skeptic sees routed bot volume on
   pools that cannot close, and the astrologer sees a market argument where
   the page promised a cultural one. Fix the scope to Pump.fun and take the
   twelve off the shared scale.
2. **Who made the coins and who "we" are (E1, H3, H15, K19, N5).** "Appeared",
   "the community built", "the people who made these coins", "more than two
   years making the case". The journalist reads evasion, the skeptic finds
   the single creating wallet, the astrologer finds a site "maintained by
   automated systems". One plain sentence about who created the coins and
   who the company is would settle all five rows.
3. **The page says it has been checked more than it has (A6, H10, I9, K4, K9,
   K27, L3, N10).** "Evidence checked 10 Nov 2026" sits over figures from
   July, a snapshot series that stopped in August, a redirect that goes
   somewhere else and labels that are not there. Each reader finds a
   different stale item, and each concludes the same thing.
4. **"We kept the critics" (G15, G18, G20, L10, L12, L14).** The record holds
   a defender's reply and a search link. All three readers can click the
   receipt, and all three see that it is not what the sentence says.
5. **Stretching a true fact past what it supports (C4, C5, D5, D6, D12,
   H6).** "Known by nearly everyone for 2,400 years", "Few say they believe",
   "the most dependable material on social media", "People come for the
   astrology". The astrologer objects on history, the journalist on sourcing,
   the skeptic on the missing numbers.
6. **Completeness claims (K6, K12, K15, K20, L7).** "No adverse figure is
   omitted", "Project treasury: None" with a Verify link, "we hold some" with
   no amount. The skeptic and journalist hit this hard; the astrologer meets
   it as a general sense that the frank tone is not carried through.
7. **False precision (F8, F9).** Millisecond ingress times that are off by up
   to a minute. The astrologer hits it first; the other two meet it as one
   more sign that the numbers are decoration.

## What all three would share

The risk paragraph, the Portnoy story with its address match, the YouGov
pair, and the missed-deadline notice. The page is strongest where it states
a checkable fact against its own interest and leaves it alone.
