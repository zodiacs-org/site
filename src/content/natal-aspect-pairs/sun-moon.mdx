Sun-Moon aspects in a birth chart describe the angle between the Sun and the Moon at the moment a person was born. This is a natal reading: both positions come from one chart, one person and one instant. In modern Western astrology the Sun stands for direction, the thing a person is building and wants to be recognized for, and the Moon stands for habit, the responses and comforts that settle them. The aspect describes how the two meet.

This angle is also the phase of the Moon. The Moon's light changes because its angle from the Sun changes, so the aspect records the phase on the birth day: a conjunction falls near a new Moon, a square near a quarter Moon and an opposition near a full Moon. The astronomy is a fact about the sky; the readings below come from the astrological tradition.

The Moon moves about 13 degrees a day through the zodiac, and about 12 compared with the Sun, which moves about one. That makes this aspect more sensitive to the birth time than most. The panels after the five sections give the limits the calculator uses and show how far the angle moves in a single day.

<h2 id="conjunction">Sun conjunct Moon in a birth chart</h2>

A Sun-Moon conjunction places the two lights close together on the wheel, usually in the same sign. In the sky this is the new Moon, when the Moon sits between the Earth and the Sun and shows no lit face. Direction and habit start from one place: what the person wants to do and what feels natural often look the same.

The traditional reading treats this as a concentrated chart. Purpose and comfort reinforce each other, which can make a person steady and single-minded. The same overlap can make it hard to tell a chosen aim from a familiar reflex.

Consider an ordinary week. The project the person cares about often becomes their routine, and the routine starts to stand in for the project. That can be productive, and it can mean rest only comes once work is finished. Notice whether there is a way to recover that is not also a way of moving a goal forward.

The sign and house matter more here than for any other Sun-Moon angle, because both lights share them. The calculator also marks whether the Moon is still closing on the Sun (applying) or has just passed it (separating), a detail some readers weigh and others leave aside.

<h2 id="sextile">Sun sextile Moon in a birth chart</h2>

A sextile puts the Sun and the Moon about 60 degrees apart, two signs away from each other. In the sky this is a crescent Moon: the thin evening crescent a few days after the new Moon, or the morning crescent a few days before it. The two lights sit in signs that usually get along, fire with air or earth with water, without being the same.

The traditional reading calls the sextile an opportunity. Direction and habit are different enough to be distinct and similar enough to cooperate, so small routines can carry larger aims if the person sets them up. Opportunity here means an opening that takes a little effort to use.

Picture someone who wants to write more. The sextile suggests a practical arrangement between plan and habit: the morning coffee becomes the time for a page, or a regular walk becomes the time to think a problem through.

The sextile is the smallest of the five major angles, and the site counts it within 5 degrees when the Sun or Moon is involved. Because the Moon moves quickly, a chart can show a sextile at one hour and none a few hours later, so check the orb and the birth time first. It is also mild and easy to miss: ask where plan and habit already help each other a little.

<h2 id="square">Sun square Moon in a birth chart</h2>

A square places the two lights about 90 degrees apart, three signs from each other. In the sky this is a quarter Moon, half lit: the first quarter about a week after the new Moon, or the last quarter about a week before it. The signs involved usually share a mode: cardinal, fixed or mutable. They differ in element, so they act in the same manner for different reasons.

The traditional reading calls the square a tension. The Sun pursues a direction, the Moon asks for a familiar comfort, and the two requests often arrive at the same time. The plan says keep going, the mood says stop, and neither is simply wrong.

Take someone who starts a demanding course of study that matters to them. Their Moon wants evenings that feel known and quiet, and each week the two collide over the same hours. The square does not say who should win. It describes a question that keeps coming back until it gets a practical answer, such as a fixed night off, a smaller workload or a different place to study.

Many astrologers say squares are where a chart does its work: adjustment practiced over years becomes knowing when to push and when to rest. The site counts a square within 8 degrees when the Sun or Moon is involved, and one close to exact usually gets more weight. Read from a noon chart, a loose square may not have been a square at the real hour.

<h2 id="trine">Sun trine Moon in a birth chart</h2>

A trine places the two lights about 120 degrees apart, four signs from each other and usually in the same element. In the sky this is a gibbous Moon, more than half lit, in the days either side of the full Moon. The signs share a temperament, fire with fire or water with water, so the two functions speak a similar language.

The traditional reading calls the trine harmonious. What a person aims at and what comforts them tend to point the same way, and moving between effort and rest may feel unforced. The tradition adds that ease is a condition, not an achievement: a trine describes a smooth path and says nothing about whether it is used.

Consider someone who likes their work and likes their evenings, and finds the two rarely compete. That is the trine at its plainest. A fair question is how unfamiliar the gap would feel if a new job or a move separated the plan from the comfort.

Next to a square, which keeps asking for adjustment, the trine rarely asks for anything, so it is easy to overlook. A close trine between the lights is often read as a steady background that makes harder aspects elsewhere feel more manageable. The site counts a trine within 8 degrees when the Sun or Moon is involved.

<h2 id="opposition">Sun opposite Moon in a birth chart</h2>

An opposition places the two lights about 180 degrees apart, on opposite sides of the wheel. In the sky this is the full Moon, when the Earth sits between the Sun and the Moon and the Moon's face is fully lit. The signs face each other, fire across from air or earth across from water, read in the tradition as complementary.

The traditional reading treats the opposition as awareness through contrast. Direction and habit face each other, so a person may see each one clearly by its difference from the other. That can feel like being pulled two ways, or like perspective.

Someone may have a public role they care about and a private life that restores them, and the two seem to ask for opposite things: visibility and retreat, schedule and rest. The opposition invites balance rather than choice. A useful reflection is whether time moves between the two in a way that feels chosen, or whether one side keeps crowding out the other.

Astrologers often note that people first meet one side of an opposition in someone close to them. That is a suggestion from the tradition, and it assigns no one a role. The site counts an opposition within 10 degrees when the Sun or Moon is involved, as wide as the conjunction. Keep the measured orb beside the full-Moon story: one near the limit is a weaker theme than one within a degree or two.

## Reading Sun-Moon in your own chart

Start with the facts the calculator gives: which angle, the exact orb and whether the birth time is known. Then look at the sign and house of each light. The aspect describes how they meet; the signs and houses describe what each one is like and where it tends to show up.

Give the tightest aspects the most weight. A Sun-Moon angle within a degree or two is usually central to a reading; one near its limit is usually a supporting detail. Other aspects to either light change the picture too.

These readings describe a pattern to reflect on, not a verdict on character or a forecast of what happens. The person decides whether they recognize it, and in what form, and "not really" is a fair answer.
