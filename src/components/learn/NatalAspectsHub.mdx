The five guides above explain each angle by itself: what a square is, how a trine differs from a sextile, how close two bodies must be to count as conjunct. A planet pair page does the other half of the job. It takes two named bodies in one birth chart, such as the Sun and the Moon, and reads all five angles between them on one page, so the same pair can be compared across geometries without opening five different pages.

These references begin with three pairs. Sun-Moon sets a person's sense of direction beside their habits and comforts, and its angle is also the phase of the Moon on the birth day. Moon-Venus sets comfort beside taste and value. Venus-Mars sets what a person values beside how they go after it. Each page has a section for every major aspect, using the same headings a reader would search for, such as "Sun square Moon in a birth chart", so an aspect listed in a calculator result is easy to find on the page.

## How the calculator decides an aspect is there

An aspect is counted when the shortest separation between two bodies falls within a set distance, the orb, of one of the five exact angles. The birth chart calculator allows wider orbs when the Sun or the Moon is involved: 10 degrees for a conjunction or opposition, 8 for a square or trine and 5 for a sextile, against 8, 7 and 4 for other pairs. That is why Sun-Moon and Moon-Venus can show an aspect that the same separation would not produce for Venus and Mars. Each pair page prints the limits it uses, and links back to the geometry guides here for the definitions.

Orbs are a convention, not a measurement of strength. Other astrologers and programs choose different limits, and a contact just outside one of them is not a missing quality in a chart. The calculator also marks each aspect as applying or separating, depending on whether the angle is closing toward exact or widening. Some readers give that distinction weight; others treat it as detail.

## Why the birth time matters more for some pairs

The Moon moves about 13 degrees a day, the Sun and Venus about one, and Mars less. A pair that includes the Moon can therefore gain, lose or change an aspect within a single day, and a chart read from a noon reference may not show what the real hour would. A pair of slow planets like Venus and Mars barely moves in a day, so the birth hour almost never changes its aspect, though many people born in the same few weeks share it. Each pair page shows a computed case of an aspect that holds for only part of its date, with the hourly figures, so the limits are concrete rather than general.

## One chart, not two

Everything in this section is natal: both bodies come from one person's chart. Comparing one person's Moon with another person's Venus is a different reading between two charts, with its own conventions, and the compatibility tool calculates those contacts. A natal aspect describes a pattern within one person to reflect on. It does not describe anyone else, and it does not forecast how things turn out.

## Using a pair page

Start with your own chart, if you have a birth time. Note which of the three pairs form an aspect, the exact orb and whether the time is known. Then open the pair and read the section for that angle, along with the method and birth-time panels. If more than one of the three pairs is present, start with the one whose orb is smallest; the tradition usually gives the tightest aspects the most weight, and a loose aspect near its limit is usually a supporting detail rather than a theme. The readings are suggestions from the astrological tradition. Keep what you recognize, and leave the rest; the computed angle stays the same either way.
